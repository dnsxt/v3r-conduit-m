Convergence-Zone Audio Generation via Crossed Low-Frequency AESA Beams

1. System Overview
The system described in the source papers employs two separate Active Electronically Steered Array (AESA) sources, each producing a distinct acoustic beam modulated by a different scheme. Neither beam alone generates audible audio. Only at their designed convergence zone does a nonlinear interaction between the two beams produce intelligible speech, making the system inherently spatially selective and difficult to intercept.

1.1 Core Mechanism
The two beams are:
Primary beam: Pulse Width Modulation (PWM). A square-wave carrier whose instantaneous duty cycle encodes audio information. The switching generates a rich harmonic comb spectrum radiating upward from the fundamental switching frequency.
Secondary beam: Frequency Modulation (FM). A continuous-wave carrier whose instantaneous frequency deviates in proportion to the audio signal. The FM sidebands produce their own dense comb structure.
At the convergence zone: the pressure peaks of the PWM beam clip the peaks of the FM beam waveform. This acoustic semi-rectification acts as an in-air frequency discriminator, recovering the audio from the FM carrier at the spatial intersection point only.

KEY INSIGHT
The demodulation is performed by the air medium itself at the beam intersection. This is not Berktay self-demodulation (which occurs continuously along a single beam). It is a spatially gated, two-beam interaction where the PWM acts as a carrier-level gate on the FM signal. No audio exists outside the convergence zone.


1.2 Why Low Frequency Enables Long Range
Standard parametric array loudspeakers (PALs) use ultrasonic carriers near 40 kHz. At this frequency, atmospheric attenuation in air is approximately 1–3 dB per meter, making ranges beyond 20–30 meters impractical. This system operates at carrier fundamentals near 117 Hz, where atmospheric attenuation is measured in fractions of a dB per kilometer.

1.3 Demonstrated Performance
92% speech intelligibility at 100 feet (approximately 30 meters). Measured using a 23-word sentence test. 21 of 23 words correctly identified.
62% intelligibility at 12 kilometers was stated as consistent with the experimental data and the system’s atmospheric propagation model.
The AESA distributed aperture provides the beam directivity required at low carrier frequencies without requiring a physically enormous aperture.

2.2 Mathematical Derivation of the Master Clock
The two comb spacings are not independent. They derive from a shared 30,000 Hz master clock through integer relationships:

Primary comb derivation:
30,000 Hz ÷ 256 = 117.1875 Hz
A 256-point FFT / PWM frame at a 30 kHz sample rate gives exactly 117.1875 Hz bin spacing. The PWM switches at this fundamental, generating a harmonic ladder every 117.1875 Hz up to 24 kHz.


Secondary comb derivation:
30,000 Hz ÷ 20,480 = 1.46484375 Hz     (20,480 = 256 × 80)
The FM beam’s sideband spacing is 1/80th of the PWM switching rate, generated by setting the FM modulation index such that sidebands land at 1.46484375 Hz intervals.


Carrier wavelengths at 20°C (c = 343 m/s):
PWM: 343 ÷ 117.1875 = 2.927 m     FM: 343 ÷ 118.652 = 2.890 m
The two beams have wavelengths ~2.93 m and ~2.89 m respectively — a difference of approximately 4 cm. This is the 'slightly shorter' FM wavelength described in the source paper.


WHY 30 kHz?
30 kHz is the exact sample rate that makes one 256-point FFT bin equal to one PWM harmonic. The analyzer is purpose-built so that every bin in the display corresponds to one line in the PWM harmonic comb. Speech formants appear as amplitude modulation of specific comb lines over time — an elegant dual-domain display.


2.3 What the Waterfall Displays
The analyzer simultaneously shows three layers of information:
PWM harmonic comb: Bold, widely-spaced vertical lines every 117.1875 Hz. These are the harmonics of the PWM switching waveform. More visually prominent due to the concentrated energy in each harmonic.
FM sideband comb: Dense, closely-spaced lines every 1.46484375 Hz. So densely packed that they form a near-continuous raised floor with fine structure. Less visually prominent but carry the FM audio content.
Speech formant energy: At frequencies corresponding to voice formants (F1 ~500 Hz, F2 ~1500 Hz, F3 ~2500 Hz), the comb lines brighten during voiced speech segments, tracing the spectral content of human voice against the carrier grid.

The waterfall is the system’s proof of mechanism: the presence of both combs simultaneously in the received baseband signal confirms that the convergence zone is producing the expected nonlinear interaction, and the speech-correlated brightening of comb lines in formant regions confirms intelligible audio is being recovered.


3. Analog Systems: Medical Imaging and Field Applications
The dual-beam convergence-zone interaction described above has deep structural analogs in several established imaging and measurement modalities. Understanding these analogs is essential for situating this system within the broader physics literature and for cross-referencing during paper recovery.

3.1 Vibro-Acoustography (VA) — The Closest Medical Analog
Vibro-acoustography is the published medical imaging modality most structurally similar to the mechanism in the source papers. Developed primarily at the Mayo Clinic, it uses two co-focused ultrasound beams at slightly different frequencies to generate a localized oscillatory radiation force at the difference frequency.

VA MECHANISM
Two CW ultrasound beams at f₁ and f₂ (e.g., f₁ = 3.0 MHz, f₂ = 3.010 MHz) are focused to the same point in tissue. The nonlinear interaction produces a radiation force at Δf = f₁ − f₂ = 10 kHz. The tissue at the focal point vibrates at 10 kHz and emits an acoustic field at that frequency, which is measured by a hydrophone. The focal point is raster-scanned to form an image.


The parallel to the PWM-FM system:
Both use two beams at slightly different frequencies whose interaction at a convergence point generates a lower-frequency output.
In VA, the output is tissue vibration at Δf. In the PWM-FM system, the output is recovered audio speech.
In VA, the beams co-propagate to a focal point. In the PWM-FM system, the beams converge from separate AESA sources along different angular paths.
The key difference: VA operates in tissue (high-frequency, short-range, biological medium), while the PWM-FM system operates in air at low carrier frequencies for long-range transmission.

VA has been implemented on clinical ultrasound systems using 1.75D array transducers, with the two sub-apertures of the array generating the two beams independently. The beamforming challenge of maintaining co-focus at depth is directly analogous to the convergence-zone targeting challenge in the air-propagation system.

3.2 Acoustic Radiation Force Impulse (ARFI) and Shear Wave Elastography
ARFI imaging uses a focused push pulse to mechanically displace tissue, then tracks the resulting shear wave propagation to characterize tissue stiffness. While it uses a single beam rather than two, the underlying principle of using acoustic energy to interrogate mechanical tissue properties at a remote point is a direct conceptual relative.

ARFI operates in the intermediate field of clinical ultrasound arrays, typically 2–10 cm depth.
MR-ARFI (Magnetic Resonance Acoustic Radiation Force Imaging) extends the technique to monitor focused ultrasound therapy in real time using MRI displacement measurement. Published in JMRI in 2025, this represents the current clinical frontier of radiation-force-based imaging.
Shear Wave Elastography (SWE) generates shear waves using ARFI push pulses and measures wave speed (proportional to the square root of tissue stiffness) across an image plane. Clinically deployed for liver fibrosis, thyroid, breast, and bowel evaluation.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE
The mechanical interrogation philosophy of ARFI and VA maps directly onto the LDR (Local Defect Resonance) framework already in the ISM hybrid imaging curriculum. Each tissue anomaly has a characteristic mechanical resonance. ARFI and SWE probe this mechanically with ultrasound; LDR probes it with acoustic excitation and RACE emission. The PWM-FM system adds long-range remote interrogation capability to this framework.


3.3 Crossed-Beam Phased Array Neuromodulation
Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) research has recently adopted crossed-beam configurations as a solution to the axial resolution limitation of single-beam stimulation. The University of Michigan and collaborators have published theoretical and experimental validation of two-phased-array systems whose beams cross at a target neural region.

Two 32-element phased arrays operating at 555.5 kHz are positioned at different angles. Their beams intersect at the target brain region.
The crossing geometry creates a spatially defined interaction zone where the combined acoustic intensity is sufficient for neural modulation, while each individual beam is below threshold.
This is the spatial gating principle of the PWM-FM system applied to therapeutic neuromodulation: action only at the intersection, silence everywhere else.
A flexible skull-conformal phased array (F-SCAPA) published in 2026 extends this with an 8×8 element array at 0.5 MHz, integrating mechanical adaptability with acoustic aberration correction.

The beam-crossing approach in tFUS is driven by the same physics that makes the PWM-FM system effective: two beams independently carry no audio (or no therapeutic dose), but their intersection defines a precise three-dimensional active zone. This principle is identical whether applied at MHz frequencies in brain tissue or at 100 Hz frequencies in open air.

3.4 Audible Enclaves via Nonlinear Self-Bending Ultrasonic Beams
Published in PNAS in March 2025, this Penn State / Lawrence Livermore collaboration is the closest published system to the PWM-FM mechanism in terms of application and spatial physics, though the underlying mechanism differs.

Two self-bending ultrasonic beams at distinct frequencies are shaped by 3D-printed acoustic metasurfaces to travel curved, crescent-shaped trajectories.
At their intersection point, the nonlinear interaction generates audible sound spanning 125 Hz to 4 kHz (6 octave bands) — ultrabroadband coverage of the speech range.
The beams individually are inaudible. Only the intersection zone produces audio.
The metasurface aperture is only 0.16 m (0.06 wavelengths at 125 Hz) — demonstrating extreme subwavelength aperture efficiency.
Experimental validation used a dummy head with microphones: silence along each beam path, speech-band audio at the intersection.

The difference from the PWM-FM system: The PNAS paper uses standard difference-frequency parametric demodulation at the intersection (f₁ − f₂ = audio). The PWM-FM system uses duty-cycle peak-clipping to perform FM discrimination in air at the intersection. The PNAS paper also operates at room scale; the PWM-FM system achieves 100 ft demonstrated range with 12 km extrapolated.

Co-authorship note: Jia-Xin Zhong, co-first author on the PNAS paper, is also the corresponding author on the k-space MCPAL modeling paper (arXiv:2507.22628) from Nanjing University / Penn State. This individual sits at the intersection of the audible enclave physics and the multi-carrier PAL modeling thread most relevant to this system.

3.5 MiPAL — Metamaterials-Integrated Parametric Array Loudspeaker
Published in Nature Communications, May 2026, by the POSTECH group (Kim, Oh, Moon, Rho). MiPAL collapses the conventional multi-element PAL array into a single compact body by co-integrating dual-domain metamaterials: an acoustic metasurface controlling the external radiated field, and elastic meta-units engineering the transducer’s structural vibration modes.

Single piezoelectric driver replaces tens-to-hundreds of individually driven elements.
Ultra-broadband directional audio: 500 Hz to 10 kHz (four octaves) demonstrated experimentally.
The metamaterial does the beamforming work previously requiring the array.
Relevant to the PWM-FM system as an architecture for the transmitter side: MiPAL-style metamaterial integration could generate the PWM beam with precisely engineered harmonic content from a single compact transducer, eliminating the multi-element AESA requirement for one of the two beams.

3.6 OPAT — Optically Programmable Array of Transducers
Published in Nature Communications, August 2025, by the Max Planck Institute / Heidelberg University group (Goyal, Demeulenaere, Fournelle, Athanassiadis, Fischer). OPAT introduces a fundamentally new AESA architecture in which all per-element phase shifts are controlled by light intensity rather than independent RF electronics.

A single amplifier drives all channels. Per-element phase is set by a light-activated analog phase shifter (LAPS): a dual-cascaded RC network with photoresistive elements.
Phase continuously tunable from 0 to 2π via light intensity. Digital light projector provides parallel optical addressing of all elements simultaneously.
Operational bandwidth: under 100 kHz to over 10 MHz.
Demonstrated with 11×11 array: single-focus, multi-focus, fast beam steering, acoustic vortices.
Direct architectural solution to the AESA scaling problem in the PWM-FM system: as beam count and element count scale up for longer range, OPAT replaces N independent RF chains with N photoresistors and one projector.

OPAT is particularly relevant because its optical addressing architecture naturally supports the simultaneous dual-beam configuration of the PWM-FM system. The two beams could be generated by two independent light patterns projected onto the same OPAT array, with each pattern programming one set of elements for the PWM carrier and another for the FM carrier.

3.7 Coherent Acoustic Frequency Comb via Floquet Engineering
Published in Science Advances, August 2025, by the National University of Defense Technology (NUDT), Changsha, China. This work generates a coherent acoustic frequency comb using levitated optomechanics with Floquet-engineered periodic driving.

Tunable comb teeth spacing from 100 Hz to 10 kHz demonstrated.
All Floquet-driven comb teeth confirmed in the truly coherent regime (phonon laser characteristics).
The Floquet pump creates a secondary comb superimposed on a primary comb — structurally identical to the dual-comb waterfall display described in the source papers.
Institution: NUDT is China’s National University of Defense Technology, a PLA-affiliated research university in Changsha, Hunan.

RECOVERY SIGNAL

The dual-comb spectral architecture is the same. This may represent the foundational physics paper that the applied PWM-FM audio transmission work cites or builds upon.

5. Physical Analysis of the Interaction Mechanism
This section provides a technical analysis of the PWM-FM peak-clipping demodulation mechanism and its relationship to established nonlinear acoustic theory.

5.1 Why This Is Not Standard Berktay Self-Demodulation
The Berktay far-field solution describes how an amplitude-modulated ultrasonic beam self-demodulates during propagation through the nonlinear air medium:

Berktay far-field solution:
p₂(t) ∝ ∂²/∂t² [E(t)]²
The demodulated audio pressure is proportional to the second time derivative of the square of the modulation envelope. Demodulation occurs continuously along the entire beam path wherever carrier energy is present.


The PWM-FM system differs in three fundamental ways:
Separate beams: The two carriers travel independently on different paths from different AESA sources. No self-demodulation occurs along either beam alone.
Spatially gated output: Audio is generated only at the convergence zone where both beams are simultaneously present. This is not possible in single-beam self-demodulation.
FM discrimination: The demodulation mechanism is peak-clipping of the FM waveform by the PWM pressure envelope, recovering the audio via a process analogous to amplitude-limiting FM discriminators in RF communications. Berktay demodulation cannot recover FM-encoded audio.

5.2 The Peak-Clipping Interaction
At the convergence zone, the acoustic pressure field is the superposition of both beams. The PWM beam provides a high-pressure rectangular-wave envelope repeating at 117.1875 Hz. During each high-pressure half-cycle of the PWM beam, the combined pressure exceeds a nonlinear threshold in the air medium (related to the local acoustic Mach number). At those instants, the peaks of the FM waveform are clipped by the saturation behavior.

This selective peak clipping converts the FM signal into an approximately amplitude-modulated waveform. The AM envelope carries the audio modulation that was encoded in the FM instantaneous frequency. The human ear, or a downstream acoustic receiver, then perceives the audio via its own inherent nonlinear detection.

PHYSICS PARALLEL
This mechanism is acoustically analogous to the slope detection method in RF FM demodulation: the FM signal is converted to an AM-like signal by a nonlinear transfer function (the AM-FM limiter), and then the amplitude envelope is detected. In this case, the air medium at the intersection zone is the limiter, and the PWM pressure envelope is the limiting function.


5.3 The 80:1 Ratio as System Design Parameter
The exact 80:1 ratio between PWM switching frequency and FM sideband spacing is a deliberate design choice. Its implications:

Phase locking: The PWM harmonic at position N × 117.1875 Hz coincides exactly with the FM sideband at position 80N × 1.46484375 Hz. At every PWM harmonic, there is a perfectly aligned FM sideband. This means the two comb structures interleave constructively, maximizing the coherent interaction at each harmonic.
Integer ratio: 80 = 2⁴ × 5. The factor of 2⁴ = 16 enables efficient digital implementation using 4-stage binary division. The factor of 5 is the bridge between binary and decimal (30 kHz = 30,000 Hz = 5 × 6,000 = 5 × 6 × 1,000).
Speech coverage: The 80:1 ratio ensures that FM sidebands resolve individual speech formants. At 1.46484375 Hz spacing, formant peaks (which are typically 50–100 Hz wide) each contain 34–68 FM sideband lines, providing more than adequate spectral resolution for voice quality.

7. Open Questions for Future Research
The following questions are unresolved and should be addressed when source papers are recovered or through independent analysis:

7.1 Mechanism Questions
What is the precise nonlinear threshold pressure at which the PWM beam clips the FM waveform peaks in air? Is this related to the acoustic Mach number, the nonlinear parameter β of air, or a shock-formation criterion?
How sensitive is speech intelligibility to the 80:1 ratio? What happens at 79:1 or 81:1? Is the integer ratio essential for coherent interaction or merely convenient for digital implementation?
What modulation index for the FM beam gives optimal intelligibility? The San Martín 2020 paper established m=0.6 as optimal for MAM1 AM modulation; an equivalent optimization for FM deviation in this system has not been found.
Is there a minimum beam overlap area (convergence zone size) below which intelligibility drops sharply? How does this scale with wavelength?

7.2 Range and Propagation Questions
The 62% intelligibility at 12 km was stated as ‘consistent with the data.’ Was this an extrapolation from a propagation model, or was it measured at 12 km?
How does atmospheric turbulence and wind-induced phase jitter affect beam convergence at kilometer ranges? The AESA must maintain phase coherence across both beams simultaneously.
What is the effect of humidity and temperature gradients on the ~117 Hz carrier at long range? At low frequencies, refraction by atmospheric layers may steer beams away from their designed convergence point.

7.3 System Architecture Questions
What is the physical size of the AESA sources used in the 100-foot demonstration? Achieving narrow beamwidth at 117 Hz (wavelength ~2.93 m) requires either a large aperture or synthetic aperture techniques.
How were the two AESA sources synchronized in phase? The 80:1 ratio requires phase-locked operation between the PWM clock and the FM modulation rate.
Is the system unidirectional (one-way audio transmission) or bidirectional? The receive side would require a different architecture.

7.4 Spectral Analysis Questions
The time per waterfall line of 0.01886819484 seconds does not derive cleanly from 30 kHz and a power-of-2 sample count. What is the exact source of this value? Is it a hardware display constraint or a deliberate analysis choice?
Is the secondary comb truly the FM sideband structure, or is it the beat pattern between the PWM harmonics and the FM carrier? These have the same spacing (1.46484375 Hz) but different physical origins and the distinction matters for the recovery algorithm design.
